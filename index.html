Three ways a case can end

Choosing the process matters as much as choosing the lawyer. It determines the cost, the timeline, and how much of your private life becomes a matter of public record. All three routes stay open until you commit to one.

Route one

Negotiated settlement

Counsel exchange financial disclosure and negotiate directly, then present a marital settlement agreement and parenting plan for the court to approve. Most cases end here, including cases that begin badly.

Fits when both sides will disclose honestly and the disputes are about terms, not facts.

Route two

Collaborative process

Both spouses and both attorneys sign an agreement not to litigate, then work through the issues in joint meetings, bringing in a neutral financial professional or child specialist where it helps. Nothing is filed to be argued over, and nothing said in the process becomes evidence.

Fits when you will co-parent for years and want the relationship intact at the end.

Route three

Litigation

When a spouse hides assets, refuses to disclose, will not follow temporary orders, or when a child's safety is at stake, the courtroom is the remedy — with discovery tools, subpoenas, and a judge who decides.

Fits when agreement is impossible or the other side is not dealing in good faith.

Practice areas

Family law matters rarely arrive one at a time. These issues are usually decided together, in a single case, under the Illinois Marriage and Dissolution of Marriage Act.

Dissolution of marriage

Illinois is a no-fault state: irreconcilable differences is the only ground. The case is rarely about whether the marriage ends — it is about the terms on which it ends.

750 ILCS 5/401

Legal separation

For spouses living apart who want support and parenting terms decided without dissolving the marriage, whether for religious, insurance, or personal reasons.

750 ILCS 5/402

Parental responsibilities

What used to be called custody is now allocated as decision-making authority over education, health, religion, and extracurriculars — by category, jointly or to one parent.

750 ILCS 5/602.5

Parenting time & relocation

A parenting plan sets the schedule, holidays, transportation, and communication between households. Moving a child's primary residence has its own notice rules and standards.

750 ILCS 5/602.7, 5/609.2

Child support

Illinois uses an income shares model: support is calculated from both parents' net incomes and the number of overnights, with health insurance, childcare, and extracurricular costs handled separately.

750 ILCS 5/505

Maintenance

Whether maintenance is awarded at all comes first; a statutory formula then generally governs amount and duration, with duration tied to the length of the marriage. Higher-income cases can fall outside the guidelines.

750 ILCS 5/504

Property & business interests

Marital property is divided equitably, which does not mean equally. Closely held businesses, professional practices, deferred compensation, and commingled or inherited assets require classification, tracing, and often valuation.

750 ILCS 5/503

Prenuptial & postnuptial agreements

Agreements made before or during a marriage that settle property and support in advance — drafted to withstand the disclosure and unconscionability challenges that surface years later.

750 ILCS 10/

Post-decree & enforcement

Support and parenting terms can be modified on a substantial change in circumstances, and enforced by petition or contempt when a party simply stops complying with the judgment.

750 ILCS 5/510, 5/610.5

How a case moves

Every matter is different, but the shape is usually the same. Knowing which stage you are in makes the process considerably less disorienting.

  1. Consultation

    An assessment of the marriage, the finances, the children, and the realistic range of outcomes — before any filing decision is made.

  2. Filing & temporary relief

    A petition opens the case. Where support, exclusive possession, or a parenting schedule cannot wait, temporary orders put terms in place for the duration.

  3. Financial disclosure

    Both sides exchange a sworn financial affidavit and supporting documents. This stage establishes what there is to divide, and it decides most cases.

  4. Resolution or trial

    Most matters conclude in a marital settlement agreement and parenting plan. What cannot be resolved is tried, and the judge decides it.

Common questions

General information about Illinois law, not legal advice about your situation. Statutes and their application change; how any of this bears on your case depends on facts a consultation would cover.

What does the first consultation cost?

Nothing. The initial consultation is free and carries no obligation to hire the firm. It is a working meeting: what the assets and debts look like, what the parenting situation is, what the realistic range of outcomes is, and what the next step should be — even if that step is to wait. How long does a divorce take in Illinois?

An uncontested case with a full agreement can conclude in months. A contested case involving a business, disputed parenting, or incomplete disclosure takes considerably longer, and the schedule depends on the county and the assigned calendar. The largest single variable is how quickly both sides produce complete financial documents.

Do I need a reason to file?

No. Illinois abolished fault grounds. Irreconcilable differences is the only ground, and a period of separation can establish it conclusively. Marital misconduct is generally not a basis for dividing property or awarding maintenance, though conduct affecting the children or the marital estate can matter for other purposes.

How is property divided?

Property is first classified as marital or non-marital. Marital property is then divided in just proportions after considering statutory factors — the length of the marriage, each spouse's contributions and economic circumstances, obligations from a prior marriage, and others. Equitable does not mean equal, and non-marital property that has been commingled may require tracing.

Who decides where the children live?

The parents, if they can agree on a parenting plan; the court, if they cannot. The standard is the best interests of the child, applied through statutory factors that include each parent's participation in past decision-making, the child's needs and adjustment, and each parent's willingness to support a relationship with the other.

Is the collaborative process right for my case?

It depends on candor. The process works when both spouses will disclose their finances fully without being compelled to, and it is a poor fit where assets are being hidden, where there is a history of coercion, or where a party will not honor an interim agreement. Because collaborative attorneys agree to withdraw if the case goes to court, choosing it and then abandoning it is expensive — which is why the fit is assessed honestly at the outset.

Can support or parenting time be changed later?

Yes, on a showing of a substantial change in circumstances, subject to statutory limits on when parenting judgments may be modified. Enforcement is a separate track: where a party ignores a support obligation or a parenting schedule, the remedy is a petition to enforce or for contempt — not self-help.

What if I am not ready to file?

That is a common and often sensible position. A consultation is still useful: knowing what the numbers look like, what you would be entitled to, and what documents to gather puts you in a far better position whenever you decide — or if the decision is made for you.
